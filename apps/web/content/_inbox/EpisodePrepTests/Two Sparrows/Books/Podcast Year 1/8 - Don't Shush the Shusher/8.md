As I’m sure happens in a lot of venues where humans are put together in isolation for any period of time, I’m thinking “Lord of the Flies”, many of the new privets at basic training felt the need to establish some kind of dominance and pecking order. One morning, likely a Sunday as that was the day we were left for a few moments without hovering adult supervision, bunks were pushed out of the way to clear a space for fighting, well wrestling as that seemed more tolerable. I had wrestled for a short time in junior high school. Maybe saying “I wrestled” is giving myself too much credit. I never won a match. I’m not even sure if I ever scored a point. I generally spent most of the match with my face shoved into the mat trying to keep from being pinned. But I watched enough WWF (WWE) that I knew, should a miracle occur and I ever found myself as more than face rash bait, the cradle would be my signature finishing move. This is done by hooking one arm around the opponents leg, usually behind the knee, while the other arm hooks around the back of the neck. Hands are locked together while the rest of the body drives the opponent’s shoulders to the mat. It adds meaning to the phase ”balling someone up”, and I was going to do it… In my dreams… maybe.
 
I had no need, nor desire, at that time to find out where I stacked up in manly merit. I was perfectly content to be put at the bottom of the list, by default, for not even trying. I didn’t even care to watch. I simply wanted to enjoy time without drill sergeants yelling at me, rather than be associated with the certain punishment that would follow if the drill sergeant walked in on what he would certainly consider a fight in the middle of the bay. I laid on my bunk and read my book and dosed a bit, a rare luxury I was not willing to pass on. I was suddenly torn from dreams of better places when someone discovered my hide out. “Sparrow, you haven’t wrestled yet.” someone observed. ”Come on Sparrow,“ others encouraged. ”You should wrestle Lennon,” came a suggestion. The encouragements to join the fray turned to taunting that appeared would not stop until I wrestled someone. Then Privet Philips blurted “ya, come on Sparrow 'you scared.” “Fine,” I thought, “If I’m going to get crushed, I might as well get eaten by the biggest guy in the room”. I had heard said somewhere, that you should never pick a fight with the smallest kid. Even if you win, you still look like a loser for fighting with the smallest guy. Worse, if you lose you will never live down being beaten by the runt. In contrast, if you fight the biggest guy and lose, no big deal, that was to be expected, you don’t lose any credibility. In fact, the likely outcome is that you gain some respect for having the guts to go toe to toe with the brut. Plus, should the off chance occur that you actually win, you will gain status instantly, even if the big guy has no skills. “Alright Philips,” I said “I’ll take you”. I took off my blouse and hung it on the end of the bunk. I made my way past knees sticking out from bunks and Soldiers sitting on the floor to the inside of the circle of gathered spectators. Many who had been less interested in previous contests suddenly paused conversations to see the skinny kid from Idaho get pummeled by this monster. As I stared at him I knew my only chance was be to be fast. I would shoot for his legs and at least get points for a takedown before my face was inevitably smashed into the polished concrete floor. I glanced at the tree trunks that supported his frame. Even if I could get my arms around them I couldn’t lift them. It was too late to devise anything different. ”Shake hands” the self-proclaimed officiator directed. He grabbed my hand and squeezed unnecessarily long and hard, probably to intimidate me. If so, it was working. I pulled away, not because of the pain, although that would have been a good enough reason, but because if I waited any longer I would chicken out. It was now or never. Our hands released signaling the start of the match. I sprang at him like a young lion on an unsuspecting full grown water buffalo. I wrapped my arms around his legs. I struggled the get my hands to touch. Immediately sensing that it was futile, I drove my shoulder into his hips. My face disappeared into his soft pudgy waist. I dug my legs like a lineman clearing a path for a running back coming from the back field. Slowly, maybe even in true slow motion, Goliath lost balance from the surprise attack and fell like a giant redwood. His head smacked the hard floor with such force that my head ached sympathetically. “Oooohhh,” the crowd chorused. I, of course, knew only one finishing move. I spun around putting my back on his chest. I reached around his neck, my right arm not coming out the other side for its thickness. With the other arm I reached down for one leg. I could barely get my hand behind his leg and couldn’t even lift it off the ground . It wasn’t a cradle at all. Mostly I just laid on top of him, my back to his chest, with my arms stretched out. ”One, Two, Three”, the officiator counted excitedly slapping the floor with each number. He raised my hand in victory. I made one fist pump then slinked off, breathing hard, knowing better than anyone in the room how lucky I had been. I avoided eye contact and the high-fives from other privets. I brushed off the complaints from Philips that the fight had been unfair, and demands for a rematch. I ignored the challenges from other potential competitors.

==Good Strategy/Bad Strategy by Richard Rumelt, teaches us that when all the beds are pushed to the side of the room and it's Lord of the Flies time, the skill you need most is "Diagnosis". Giving it your all isn't a strategy, growing 10% isn't a strategy, even great strategies can be terrible strategies if your read of the situation is wrong. The best strategy always comes from a realistic understanding of what's actually happening.==
 
==In Homer’s case, the obvious could have been, “I have to wrestle someone now” which could lead to any number of strategies that could have lead to any number of outcome. But he looked deeper, diagnosed the situation, and realized he was in a room full of bored privates trying to build a pecking order out of sweat, bunks, and poor judgment.==
 
==Once he understood the stakes and the real game being played he knew trying to beat someone his own size or someone smaller could hurt him in the long run. If he won, nobody would care. If he lost, the story could grow teeth.==
 
==Taking on the biggest guy in the room changed the math. Losing would be expected. Winning would be epic. Either way, he avoided the worst outcome: looking small while fighting small.==
 
==Rumelt’s strategy kernel has three parts:==

1. ==Diagnosis==
2. ==Guiding policy==
3. ==Coherent action==
 
==Homer had all three, whether he knew it or not.==

1. ==His diagnosis was that this was a status problem, not a wrestling problem.==
2. ==His guiding policy was to choose the opponent whose size made the risk worth taking.==
3. ==His coherent action was to use the only advantages he had: speed, surprise, and the sudden full-body commitment of a man who very much did not want a second thought.==
 
==In any given situation you may win, you may lose, you may be lucky, or you may be unlucky. But the strategist rigs the game, so that if he loses he's not out much, and if he wins he gets to walk away looking like he knew exactly what was going to happen the whole time.==
 
==Top dog. Charisma. Sincerity.==
 
==Take on tough challenges. Be prepared to lose. When you do lose, don't point out what everything outside your control went wrong. Win or lose sometimes the best thing you can do is keep your mouth shut. If you have to point our something point the things you did do well. Perform when it counts.==

**Don't Shush the Shusher**  
The thing I struggled with the most at Basic was not the physical or mental challenges. I struggled most with people who could not follow directions. Too many times I recall sitting in an empty room after our drill sergeant gave specific instructions to “clean weapons and be quiet.” Whispering would start, which escalated to quite talking. Then shushing would follow, followed by the shushing of the shushers. Egos got in the way of reason, and arguments ignited over who should “shut up” and who couldn’t tell who to “shut up”. "Shhhhhh." "you shhhhh" ”I don't have to shhhhh.” ”You don't tell me what to do." The drill sergeant would return, make us all do push-ups, then leave with the same instructions as before, “clean weapons and be quiet”. As soon as he left, the fighting would commence again. “We wouldn’t get smoked if you would stop talking”. “You stop talking”. “Why don’t you both stop talking” a third party would chime in. “Why don’t you butt out”. "Why don't you shut up"… It was almost a relief when the drill sergeant would come in again and we would exercise until we couldn’t possibly move any part of our bodies anymore. Then he would leave and it would start all over again. Egos and selfishness sometimes overrule reason and sound judgment. **You cannot allow pride to affect your decisions.** If you do, you will inevitably make poor decisions. You also cannot have a culture of selfishness in a successful organization. Competition? Yes! But when people feel the need to save face despite negative consequences they make rash choices and tear teams apart.

==In 1955, sociologist Erving Goffman published “On Face-work,” where he described social life as a kind of performance in which people are constantly trying to maintain the version of themselves they believe they are presenting to others. “Face” is not just vanity. It is the public self you are trying to keep alive in the room. It is your little invisible name tag that says, “I am competent, respectable, not stupid, not weak, and absolutely not someone who gets corrected by Private So-and-so while cleaning weapons.”==
 
==It sounds stupid, and honestly it is, but in the moment for the shusher it feels like actual danger.== ==￼==  
==One private talks. Another private shushes him. If the first private simply gets quiet, the problem ends. But now he has been corrected in front of everyone. His face is on the floor, blinking under fluorescent lights. So he has to pick it up. “You shhhhh.” “You don’t tell me what to do.” Suddenly the mission is no longer silence. The mission is reputation repair.==
 
==Brown and Levinson in 1987 took Goffman’s ideas further with politeness theory by describing “face-threatening acts,” which are moments in conversation that challenge someone’s desire to be approved of, respected, or left free to act. They say, “You are wrong,” and also, “You need to change what you are doing because I said so.” That is a lot of social C4 packed into one little “shhh.”==
 
==Tone and verbiage matters a ton here. According to the research:==  
==“Hey, drill sergeant said quiet,” may give the person a ladder to climb down.==  
==“Shut up,” kicks the ladder away and then seems surprised when the person starts throwing furniture.==

This was the company’s new commander, not that I had seen the old commander more than once. He was giving some “Hooah” speech that I was less than interested in. - In the Army it is customary to say “Hooah” as a response. Some argue that “Hooah” is actually ”H-U-A”, and like most things in the Army, it is an acronym which stands for Heard Understood and Acknowledged. Whatever it means, it can be somewhat motivating at times but mostly becomes droning and mindless. As I sat thinking more about saying “Check” verses “Hooah” than about what was actually being said, I got the sense that something wasn’t right. All was quiet, too quiet, and although I had heard my share of boring speeches in my life, this one seemed to be dragging out intentionally. He seemed to be stalling for something. I felt uneasy, and while the cool grass invited me to lounge on one elbow as the rest of the Soldiers did, I sat ridged looking around. As I observed, I noticed the drill sergeants in the back passing each other and shooting glances that said to me that something was definitely up but that they were trying to be discrete about it. ==Rumelt, in== _Good Strategy/Bad Strategy_==, says good strategy starts with diagnosis: figure out what kind of problem you are actually in before you start solving it. Bad strategy skips that step and reaches for effort noises. Try harder. Be bold. Give 110 percent. Please clap. Good strategy names the real problem, picks a guiding policy, and takes coherent action.==
 
==But the hiss of a CS canister is a poor soundtrack to a planning meeting.==
 
==Kahneman gives us the other half. In== _Thinking, Fast and Slow_==, he calls the slow, deliberate mind System 2 and the fast, automatic mind System 1. System 2 can read Rumelt and say “diagnosis” with a serious face. System 1 has to get your mask on when the air turns spicy. Strategy is not just making a good plan in your comfortable brain. It is preparing the uncomfortable brain before it gets summoned.==
 
==I love hearing military people tell stories about mind-bogglingly tense moments, because there is always a point where I would be screaming in bold, all-caps italics, in the most dramatic sans-serif font my voice could manage, and the soldier just turns into a checklist. “So there I was, plummeting rapidly to earth, my primary chute nothing but a wistful memory. I looked down, grabbed the emergency cord, and pulled.” Years later, the hand still goes to the cord.==
 
==That is System 1 trained by System 2.==
 
==Basic training looks like the Army trying to teach System 1 what to do before System 2 finds its pants.==  
==In the riverbed, the obvious diagnosis is boredom. The commander is giving some “Check, Sir” speech, and everyone is trying not to slide fully into the grass. But Homer notices the pattern is wrong. The speech is dragging. The drill sergeants are passing looks. The whole scene has the quiet little stink of adults setting up a lesson they are going to enjoy more than the students. Homer does not know exactly what is coming, but he knows the current explanation no longer fits.==  
==Kahneman and Gary Klein have a useful point about intuition: it is worth trusting when the environment has real patterns and you have had enough practice and feedback to learn them. That is the difference between vigilance and paranoia. Vigilance recognizes a pattern. Paranoia writes one. Homer is not having a prophecy. He is reading the room.==  
==Then the canister pops.==
 
==No meeting. No whiteboard. No time to become the person you always meant to be. Homer gets the version of himself he has already trained: case open, mask out, chin in, straps tight, clear and seal. The Army standard is nine seconds. He does it in under four.==
 
==Then he has to debug being alive. Breathe. Open your eyes. Move your hood.==  
==Stress does that. It turns the brain into a broom closet. Being prepared does not make you smooth. It makes you recoverable. It gives panic rails.==
 
==Rumelt’s kernel keeps showing up: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action. Something is off, so get ready. Gas is out, so mask first. Lennon is next to you, so check your buddy. Gear is scattered everywhere, so beat the drill sergeant to it before helmets, masks, cases, and weapons become future suffering with serial numbers.==
 
==Keep diagnosing as reality changes the assignment.==
 
==It ties the stories together too. In the wrestling story, Homer understood he was not really in a wrestling problem. He was in a status problem. In the shushing story, everybody thought they were in a noise problem, then face turned it into reputation repair. With Simmons, you get a man full of Army-adjacent information, JROTC confidence, and enough Volkswagen upholstery detail to survive three decades in Homer’s memory. But information that has not become behavior is just trivia in uniform.==
 
==Under pressure, you do not get what you know. You get what you practiced. So the cleaned-up version of top dog is not dominance. It is usefulness. Top dog is the person you are glad is sitting next to when the canister pops. The person whose fast brain has been trained by slow work. The person who notices when the game changes, gets himself squared away, checks his buddy, and still has enough left to make the situation better for somebody else.==
 
==That is care through competence. Nobody has to say anything tender. You grab the weapon. You return the helmet. You keep someone from getting destroyed later. Sometimes love looks like beating a drill sergeant to a rifle in a dry riverbed.==  
==And yes, sometimes karma arrives as a protective mask full of consequences. You do not have to be proud of laughing. But you are allowed to remember, especially if “Remember that time?” is the only sentence that ever made Simmons quiet.==
 
==Learn the job until it reaches your hands. Diagnose the real problem. Trust the bad feeling when it has earned your trust. Check your people. Do the next useful thing.==

==Bad strategy is what everyone else is doing in the earlier stories. It is wrestling the wrong person because ego picked the opponent. It is shushing the shusher because your face got dented. It is talking yourself into trouble because your mouth is faster than your judgment. Bad strategy treats the visible problem as the real problem. Good strategy asks what game is actually being played.==  
==In the wrestling story, the game was not wrestling. It was status. In the shushing story, the game was not noise. It was face. In the CS gas story, the game starts as boredom, becomes survival, becomes teamwork, then becomes saving gear before the drill sergeant turns the riverbed into a punishment orchard.==  
==And Simmons, God bless him, gives us the control group.==  
==He had information. He had JROTC. He had stories. He had a pearl-white Volkswagen Beetle with a royal purple shag interior burned so deeply into Homer’s memory that it survived three decades and probably deserves its own VA claim. But information that has not become behavior is just trivia in uniform. Under pressure, you do not get what you know. You get what you have practiced.==  
==So the lesson is not “be fearless.” Fearless is mostly something people say after the danger is over and the lighting is flattering. The lesson is: train until the useful thing happens first. Pay attention when the pattern changes. Trust the uneasy signal, but do not worship it. Check your buddy. Help people even when nobody assigned them to you.==  
==That is the cleaned-up version of top dog.==  
==Not the loudest guy. Not the biggest guy. Not the guy with the most confident wrong answer. The top dog is the person you are relieved to be sitting next to when the canister pops. The person whose fast brain has been trained by slow work. The person who can get himself squared away and still have enough left to make the situation better for somebody else.==  
==And yes, sometimes karma arrives as a protective mask full of consequences. Homer feeling bad for Simmons and also laughing is not hypocrisy. It is range.==  
==But the real win was not getting a story to hold over Simmons later, though “Remember that time?” did become a very efficient communication tool. The real win was that when the moment changed, Homer changed with it. He saw the problem, acted, checked his people, and kept moving.==  
==That is good strategy with sweat on it.==
