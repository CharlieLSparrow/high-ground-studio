On Sundays at my house, growing up in Southeast Idaho, my mom, a small, thin woman with light skin but dark eyes and short dark hair, would get out of bed at 5:00 a.m. (sleeping in, compared to the rest of the week for her). At that hour she prepared roast and potatoes for Sunday dinner - not a big surprise when you have dairy cows and potato fields, but a luxury we were not allowed to take for granted. After three hours of church, and before the cows had to be milked again, I would sit around the dark wood table on big wooden chairs with my two sisters, my brother, and mom. My dad at the head of the tables would ask each of us, in turn, what we learned at church that day. It was at those times that he often attempted to impart words of fatherly wisdom to his progeny.
 
One day as we sat around that table in the house on bend in the road, with the rust colored linoleum beneath our feet, he told of a small swallow he saw earlier that week ferociously defending her nest from a red-tailed hawk. I stared out the big dining room window across the field with hay laying in neat rows. I lost track of the story as I watched birds of all sizes discovering rodents, bugs, and other feasts newly exposed by the freshly cut crop. His story complete, he paused. The quiet pulled me from my daydream. I had missed the point of the tale. I suppose the moral of that particular story was less important than what he shared next. He looked passed me sitting at the foot of the table across from him, passed the golden Formica countertops behind me, passed the classic gold refrigerator of that late 70s home, through the door, through the carport, and out into the pastures beyond. Then he encouraged us all to look for life lessons in everyday things. It seemed pretty simple for such dramatic effect. Intrigued, and always looking to please or impress my father.
 
- [ ] I watched for months but saw nothing. I tried to come up with things at random… This banana peel is like when you… get… old… or… brown spots… that… or… even throw it away... I got nothing. Despite growing up on a small rural farm, my parents felt it important for us to be exposed to culture and the arts. Most of the events we attended were free, but culture building none-the-less. I had done it. I found a life lesson in a mundane, ordinary, everyday thing.

# Chapter Close: Awkwardness, Warmth, and Attention

This has become core to everything I’ve tried to become, to whatever degree of success or failure, as a leader and as a person.  
I was a pretty awkward kid (hold for gasps from the audience). I know, I know, I’m a pretty awkward adult too. But in spite of the struggles and anxiety it has caused in my life at times, on the whole I’m grateful for the lessons I’ve learned from being awkward and self-conscious.  
The biggest one has been that no matter how weird I feel in a given situation, if I shift the focus from myself to others it always goes better.  
Once I’m focused on the person in front of me, and trying to understand them, I almost always like them. Once it’s clear to them that I genuinely care about them and what they’re saying, something magic happens. They lean in.  
Like all of our lessons in this chapter, this idea is embarrassingly simple.  
If you pay attention to the person in front of you instead of yourself, you’ll begin to understand them.  
If you understand them, you’ll probably care about them.  
If you care about them, you’ll treat them the way they want to be treated.  
As simple as all of that is, are you doing it? As often as you should?  
Me neither, let’s work on it together.  
In _The Charisma Myth_, Olivia Fox Cabane breaks down the research around charisma to three things: Power, Presence, and Warmth.  
If you’re with someone, pay attention to them, be present with them, try to understand them. As you understand them, you’ll care more about them. That creates warmth. All of this pulls you outside of yourself, making it easier to find confidence and power.  
This is obviously oversimplified, but simple triggers that lead to healthier mindsets are what it’s all about. Sometimes the best we can be for now is a goldfish wearing a shark fin, or a sloth in a hustle t-shirt, and that’s a start!  
So being kind, working hard and enjoying it, not wasting people’s time, and listening aren’t just nice things to do. They are critical to life, work, and even to your internal world. They’re worth pursuing and remembering to practice, for their own merits and for the good they lead you to.  
Sometimes leadership is not brilliance.  
Sometimes it is not strategy.  
Sometimes it is not perfectly engineered incentives or a clearly articulated mission statement.  
Sometimes it is just this:  
Look at the person in front of you.  
Care about what they’re saying.  
And  
Just listen.

**THE SWEAR JAR** _(Text: Chapter Three - Part 5)_  
One of the first things people noticed about me at basic training was the way I talked. Bobby Little, from Boston (whose name backwards is Little, Bobby), and Privet Young from Mississippi (who had no rhythm and could not march) had more distinct accents but it was more about what I didn’t say than what I did say. I didn’t tell dirty jokes or use foul language. Many Soldiers asked me why I didn’t; arguing that they are “just words” and that “it doesn’t matter”. I didn’t argue. I agreed. They are just words, and I _just chose_ not to use them. When I began my Advanced Individual Training some of the guys that went with me to basic training joined me. I remember vividly the first Sunday morning at AIT. I heard from across the bay “SPARROW WHAT?!”, “WHERE IS HE?”, “SPARROW!” PVT Drummond, who was a huge human, came into view as I moved to the center of the room. I saw that he brought company, all the people he had established as his pose over the first few days of being there. The rest of the bay of 60 or so people gathered around to see what was going on. I didn’t know what he wanted but he sounded angry. “You Sparrow?” he glared at me. “Yep,” I said as cheerful as I dared. “Green tells me you don’t G*^ D@#& swear.” “Nope,” I replied, again as cheerfully as I thought I could get away with. PVT Drummond spent what seemed like an eternity swearing words that I had never heard, and describing in the most disgusting way how little he thought of me. Then he stopped, looked hard at me, and walked away. The crowd dispersed murmuring. I went back to what I was doing, confused about what had just happened and why. I said a silent prayer that I was still breathing out of my face and not a hole in my chest. Then I didn’t think much more about it. A few weeks later I stood in a formation with my platoon. The drill sergeants gave us instructions, as they always did, to stand still and not talk. I thought they were simple instructions, but the same meat head that never understood that “don’t talk” meant “don’t open and close your mouth while vibrating your vocal cords”, started trying to be funny and telling dirty jokes. I turned and looked at him and boldly said “shut up”. (Did I mention PVT Jones weighed in around 2 “fity” at 6’ 3”, dwarfing my 5’ 10” 145lbs with my rucksack on?) “What did you say to me?” he said shocked. I immediately regretted my approach. I had probably been too harsh, but I had already committed. “You heard what I said,’ Shut Up!’” I looked at him, “I am tired of getting smoked because you can’t keep your mouth shut and stand still.” In the Army, especially during entry level training, group punishment is the norm. If one person is out of line, everyone pays. This guy had caused us all to do any number of strenuous exercises, known as “getting smoked”, on multiple occasions for not holding his tongue. The conversation ended quickly as the drill sergeants returned. When the formation ended, I went to my bunk quickly followed by PVT Jones and the rest of the platoon. “Do you want to fight me, Sparrow?” he scowled looking down at me and puffing out his chest. I looked up and calmly replied. “No, I don’t want to fight you. There is no fight to have. You hit me one time and I wouldn't remember my name.” I hesitated, then determined that I was already in this and continued. ”I am just sick and tired of getting smoked because you can’t shut your mouth in formation.” “You’re just weak and can’t hack it,” he retorted. “I can do the push-ups,” I told him, “I can do the exercises. I just don’t want to have to, and we wouldn’t have to, if we would all just do what we’re supposed to do, and keep quiet in formation.” I knew at any minute this guy was going to feed my teeth to me. I had visions of blacking out and waking up on the floor. Then something unexpected happened. PVT Drummond stepped between us. “Come on Jones. You just can’t talk sense into this guy,” and he pushed him away. Nothing more was ever said to me about either of those incidences. But I was again grateful to have the same number of holes in my face, and Jones did tone it down a bit.
 
There was another Soldier of my faith in 44 Truck, PVT Johnson. He tried hard to fit in and make friends. He relaxed his standards. No, he didn’t do anything unconscionable, or even pick up any addictive habits. He just decided to use foul language and laugh at the off colored jokes. What happened surprised me. I became a person who was respected and admired for having principals and living by them. PVT Johnson became a laughing stock; mocked for having no discipline or backbone to follow what he claimed to be right. I don’t know that this is worth much more than a good story for anyone else. But for me it represents more than a story. It represents who I am. I couldn’t swear now if I wanted to. I used the word “pissed” to describe how upset I was in an email I sent once and I lost a little respect from the some of the foulest mouthed people. Probably writing it here got a rise, or maybe a chuckle, from a few of you. But for some, who have never heard me say it, I may have even lost a little credibility. I have a reputation to uphold. I am respected for having discipline and control of myself, including my tongue. Where does this leave me as a leader? No, I don’t have a swearing jar, nor am I advocating for it. I don’t point my finger at anyone and call them a heathen, that's not helpful and would only make me look arrogant. They just know that I don’t talk like that. Eliminating offensive language and degrading comments from the work place makes it an environment people want to work in. **Demonstrating discipline earns respect and leads to disciplined followers.**
 
After Basic Training I went home for a few months and began a job at Icon Health and Fitness making treadmills on a production line. I worked on a team with one other person. She would start the treadmill for the first time, checking functionality. I would put on the back roller and then adjust the belt. It was at my station that any issues with the machine were often discovered for the first time. When something had a problem, the machine was moved off the production line and onto the “mechanics loop”. The mechanic would fix the issue and move it back onto the line or assemble the machine alone before sending it through the quality control booth. I quickly learned that I could fix many of the issues myself without sending the half assembled machine off to be someone else's problem. I also discovered that by doing so I could save a lot of time in the production process. I found, for example, that if a bad batch of circuit boards were put into the treadmills that the workers putting in circuit boards simply continued to put them on and it took a long time before the problem was finally cut off at the source. If I identified the issue, I could stop bad circuit boards from being put in, quickly change out those already on the line and keep production running.
 
My line leaders saw in me an ability to work independently and when a position came available to bring parts to the line I was offered the parts puller job. I enjoyed very much being off the grind of the line. I also made a few more cents an hour, a big perk for me as I was working to save money. With me off the production line, the mechanic became inundated with machines. No one was willing to step outside of their wrote production script. When he became overwhelmed and quit I was offered his position. I was initially reluctant but found that I could be bought for a few more cents an hour. I simply trained a few key people on the line what things to watch out for and empowered them to fix the issues before they got to me. Things ran so smoothly that I started assisting where there were bottle necks on the line. I didn’t do all of this unselfishly. Although I do enjoy helping others and fixing processes; we worked for piece rate. That meant that the more treadmills we made in a night, the more money we made. With the production line running smooth and everyone energized to make more money, quality control seemed to be the greatest hold up. So, I again was offered a few more cents an hour to work QC. Then after only a few short months, I got to be a lead in charge of a couple people for a special project. I don’t recall exactly what the circumstances were but I do remember one of the people on my team thinking I was too harsh and crying. When the project we were working on ended I went back to quality control and was not asked to continue in a lead position. I enjoyed my job and continued to make good money for me and the team. **Seek for ways to help the team. Look outside of what you are doing to make the organization better. Often times you will be rewarded for your efforts.**

Yes. **This is a really useful Radical Candor distinction**, and it fits leadership beautifully.  
Kim Scott’s language is basically:

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|**Type**|**What they want**|**Leadership mistake to avoid**|
|**Superstars**|Growth, challenge, promotion, bigger scope|Holding them in place because they are useful where they are|
|**Rock stars**|Stability, mastery, respect, recognition|Assuming they lack ambition or need to be “fixed”|

The key is that **both can be high performers**. The difference is not talent. It is trajectory.  
A **superstar** is on a steep growth curve. They want the next mountain. More responsibility, bigger risks, broader impact. If you do not give them a path forward, they may leave, not because they are disloyal, but because the room has become too small for the engine they are running.  
A **rock star** is excellent and steady. They may not want promotion, management, politics, or a bigger title. They may want to keep doing the thing they are great at and be genuinely valued for it. The leadership danger is treating them as if they are “less ambitious” when really they may be more grounded, more self-aware, or more committed to craft.
 
**Radical Candor has a really helpful distinction here: not every high performer wants the same reward.**  
Some people are high performers because they are reaching for the next rung. They want growth, promotion, challenge, and the chance to prove they can carry more. These are the people who get restless if they are not stretched. Give them a hill, a sword, and a mildly unreasonable objective and they start humming battle music.  
But other high performers are not trying to climb out of the role. They are trying to be excellent in it. They want recognition, trust, autonomy, and respect. They do not necessarily want a bigger title or a larger headache wearing a blazer. They want someone to notice that they are holding up half the cathedral while everyone else is arguing about the color of the stained glass.  
The mistake leaders make is assuming that promotion is the only meaningful reward. For some people, it is. For others, the reward is being seen, respected, protected, and allowed to keep doing excellent work without being punished for not wanting to become a manager.  
That distinction matters because leadership is not just about identifying talent. It is about understanding what kind of future that person is trying to build. Same performance. Different mindset. Different stewardship. Different kind of care. 🪶
